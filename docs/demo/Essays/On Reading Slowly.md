# On Reading Slowly

Most of what we read now is skimmed. We scan for the sentence that answers the question we arrived with, and leave the rest behind. ==Slow reading is the opposite habit: staying with a page long enough for it to change your mind.==<!--mdr:note {"text":"Good opening line for the talk. Pair with the Seneca quote.","color":"yellow"}-->

## Why margins matter

Readers have always written in books. A margin is where a text stops being a monologue and becomes a conversation: a question mark beside a weak argument, a line under a sentence worth keeping, a note to a future self. <!--mdr:bookmark {"label":"The core argument"}-->

Taking notes *beside* the text, instead of in a separate file, keeps the thought attached to the thing that caused it. <mark data-hl="green">The annotation and its context travel together.</mark>

> To read without a pencil in hand is to daydream.

## A simple practice

1. Read a section once without stopping.
2. Go back and mark only what you would want to find again.
3. Write one note per section, <mark data-hl="pink">in your own words</mark>.<!--mdr:note {"text":"Try this with the next three chapters and compare.","color":"pink"}-->

## What to keep

| Mark | Use it for |
| --- | --- |
| Highlight | A sentence you would quote |
| Sticky note | Your reaction or a question |
| Bookmark | A place to return to |

The goal is not to collect highlights. <mark data-hl="blue">It is to leave the page having thought something you had not thought before.</mark>
